# Case Study Interview — Step-by-Step Master Guide
**Built from the "IIMA Consult Prep Book 2024-25" (Consult Club, IIM Ahmedabad)**

This guide teaches you **HOW TO THINK** in a case interview — not just how to memorise answers. It is written for a complete beginner, uses the book's own examples, and gives you interview-ready sentences you can actually say out loud.

---

## HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE
- Read **Part A** first — it is the foundation everything else builds on.
- Then work through **Part B → F** one case type at a time. Each part follows the exact same structure:
  1. Concept in simple language
  2. How to approach it in the interview (with sentences to say)
  3. What the interviewer is testing / common mistakes
  4. A real case from the book, solved step-by-step
  5. The same case as a full interview conversation
- End with **Part G** — your reusable "Thinking Template" for unseen cases.

> **Golden rule from the book (Page 29):** *"There is no one right solution for any case statement... what aspect one targets is very subjective with no right or wrong choice."* Your job is to be **structured, comprehensive, and clear** — not to find "the" answer.

# PART A — THE UNIVERSAL 4-STEP METHOD

Every single case — profitability, market entry, pricing, growth, guesstimate — is solved with the same four steps. The book calls it:

> **CLARIFY → STRUCTURE → ANALYSE → CONCLUDE**

Let me explain each in plain language, because everything else in this guide is just a richer version of these four steps.

## Step 1: CLARIFY (Understand the problem)
**What it means:** Before you do anything, make sure you and the interviewer are talking about the *same problem*. Ask questions to pin down the exact question, the client, the objective, and the numbers.

**Why it matters:** The book says (Page 11): *"a mistake committed here has the biggest potential to ruin the entire interview."* If you solve the wrong problem, everything after is wasted.

**What to ask (example sentences):**
- *"Just to confirm, our client is a beer manufacturer in India, and we're asked to find out why their costs are higher than international benchmarks — is that correct?"*
- *"What is the client's objective here — is it to grow revenue, cut costs, or improve profitability?"*
- *"Is this a company-specific problem or an industry-wide trend?"*
- *"What time period are we looking at, and how big is the change?"*

## Step 2: STRUCTURE (Break it into parts)
**What it means:** Turn one big scary question into a few small manageable pieces. You do this with a framework (a mental map). You don't need to memorise frameworks — you need to understand *why* you split the problem the way you do.

**Why it matters:** A structure shows the interviewer you can think logically and not miss anything. It also stops you from panicking — you always know what to do next.

**Example sentence:** *"I'd like to approach this by first breaking the problem into its revenue and cost components, then digging into whichever side is causing the issue. Does that sound reasonable?"*

## Step 3: ANALYSE (Do the work)
**What it means:** Now you ask targeted questions to get data, do calculations, and draw inferences. The book gives two powerful questions to ask yourself about every data point (Page 11):
- *"How did this data point look earlier for us?"* (trend over time)
- *"How does this data point look for our competitors?"* (benchmark)

**Why it matters:** Analysis is where you prove you can actually solve problems, not just talk about them. Communicate your reasoning out loud as you go — the interviewer wants to *see* your brain working.

## Step 4: CONCLUDE (Recommend)
**What it means:** End with a clear, confident, actionable recommendation — not a vague summary. Include short-term and long-term actions, and do a quick "sanity check" on any numbers.

**Why it matters:** The book says (Page 11): *"All efforts put in thus far are of little use if the candidate is unable to come up with a proper conclusion backed by a logical implementation plan."* Concrete solutions beat broad vague answers.

**Example sentence:** *"To summarise: the root cause is X. In the short term I'd recommend A, and in the longer term I'd recommend B. This should improve profitability by roughly Y%."*

---

## THE A4 SHEET TECHNIQUE (from the book, Page 11)
The book strongly recommends using a single A4 sheet to organise your thinking, because interviewers may ask to see your working sheet. Rules:
- Keep it **simple** — the interviewer should understand your approach at a glance.
- Write your structure at the top, your numbers in the middle, your conclusion at the bottom.
- Use a framework as a guide, but **never force-fit** a framework just to use it. Say what you're doing in your own words, not "I'm using the market entry framework."

# PART B — PROFITABILITY CASES
*(Book Section A — 24 cases, e.g. Beer Manufacturer, Auto Insurance, Fast Food, Restaurant)*

## 1. The concept in very simple language
**What is a profitability case?** The client's profits (or profitability) are going up/down and you must find out *why* and *what to do about it*.

**Key terms before we go on:**
- **Revenue (Income):** the money coming in from selling things. `Revenue = Price × Quantity sold`
- **Costs:** the money going out to make and sell those things.
- **Profit:** `Profit = Revenue − Costs`
- **Profitability:** profit as a *proportion* of revenue (e.g. "20% profit margin"). The book stresses that profits are merely the difference of revenue and cost, while profitability is profit as a proportion of sales.
- **Fixed Costs:** costs that don't change with how much you produce (e.g. factory rent, salaries).
- **Variable Costs:** costs that change with how much you produce (e.g. raw materials, fuel).
- **Value Chain:** the series of steps a product goes through — R&D → raw material → production → storage/transport → distribution → marketing → after-sales. Every step costs money.

**The Profitability Framework (book Pages 14-15):**
```
Profit = Revenue − Cost
Revenue = Price × Units Sold
  Units Sold = Market Size × Market Share
  (demand side: need, affordability, accessibility, availability, awareness, after-sales)
Costs → analysed along the Value Chain:
  R&D → Raw Material → Production → Storage & Transport → Distribution → Marketing → After-sales
  (each cost head: rent, electricity, machinery, labour, capacity utilisation, packaging, defects)
```

**Why we use it:** It forces you to look at *both* sides (revenue and cost) so you don't miss the real cause.

**When to use it in an interview:** When the client talks about profits, revenue, costs, margins, or profitability declining or underperforming.

## 2. HOW TO APPROACH IT IN AN INTERVIEW
Follow this sequence:

1. **Clarify:** confirm the client, the product, the geography, whether it's revenue or cost, company-specific vs industry-wide, and the time period.
   - *"Is the problem on the revenue side, the cost side, or both?"*
   - *"Is this happening to everyone in the industry, or just our client?"*
2. **Structure:** say you'll break it into Revenue and Cost, then drill into the value chain.
   - *"I'd like to structure this as: first, revenue = price × units sold; second, costs along the value chain. Let's identify which side is causing the issue."*
3. **Analyse:** ask which side is the problem, then drill into sub-heads. For costs, look at components in order of size.
4. **Conclude:** give short-term and long-term recommendations.

## 3. What the interviewer is testing & common mistakes
**What looks strong:**
- Asking clarifying questions before diving in.
- Breaking costs into a value chain so nothing is missed.
- Asking *"is this company-specific or industry-wide?"* early.
- Giving a mix of short-term and long-term recommendations.

**Common mistakes:**
- **Forcing a framework** just to use it.
- **Being mechanical** — sounding rehearsed rather than thinking.
- **Panicking after a calculation error** — instead, own it and move on.
- **Guessing randomly** — the interviewer can tell when you're not structuring. If you jump to "reduce costs" without a structure, they'll think you're guessing.
## 4. A real case from the book — "Beer Manufacturer" (Page 34, Easy)
**The interviewer presents:**
> "Your client is a beer manufacturing company that operates in India. The cost structure for the company is poor compared to international benchmarks, and you have been asked by the client to find out why."

**Step-by-step thinking (what you, the candidate, do):**

| Step | What you do | What you're thinking |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| 1 | Ask: *"Is the problem only on the cost side, or should I also analyse revenue?"* | I must confirm scope — don't waste time on revenue if it's a cost problem. |
| 2 | Ask: *"Is it one product or many? Is it industry-wide or client-specific?"* | If it's industry-wide, the cause is external (market). If it's just the client, the cause is internal (their operations). |
| 3 | **Structure:** break costs into the value chain (R&D, raw material, processing, storage & transport, distribution, marketing). | The value chain is a complete map of where money is spent — nothing gets missed. |
| 4 | Ask which cost head deviates most. → **Processing costs.** | Focus effort where the problem is, not everywhere. |
| 5 | Break processing costs into sub-heads: rent, electricity, machinery, labour/wages, technology, capacity utilisation, packaging, defects. → **Capacity utilisation.** | Each cost head can hide many causes; break it down. |
| 6 | Capacity utilisation = Actual Output / Potential Output. Current is 100%. | If actual = potential, the problem is that *potential* is too low — i.e., the machines/plants are too small. |
| 7 | Client has 3 plants, each with 30,000-bottle capacity. Advanced machines cost 1.5× and produce 2×. | So the client's *potential* capacity is limited by old machines, and they're at 100% of a low ceiling. |
| 8 | **Conclude** with short/medium/long-term recommendations. | Give an implementation plan, not just a diagnosis. |

## 5. The same case as an actual interview conversation
```
INTERVIEWER: Your client is a beer manufacturing company in India. Their cost structure is poor
             versus international benchmarks. Find out why.

ME:          Just to confirm — we're looking at the cost side only, and I should find why our
             costs are higher than international players. Is that right?

INTERVIEWER: Yes, the problem is only on the cost side.

ME:          Understood. Before I start, is this problem specific to our client, or is the whole
             industry facing it? And is it one product or across many?

INTERVIEWER: It's across many of our products, and our competitors — mostly international —
             aren't facing this issue.

ME:          That tells me the cause is internal to our client, not the market. I'd like to
             structure the analysis by breaking costs down along the value chain — R&D, raw
             material, processing, storage and transport, distribution, and marketing. Does that
             cover everything?

INTERVIEWER: The value chain seems fine.

ME:          Great. To focus my effort, which cost head deviates most from benchmark?

INTERVIEWER: Processing costs are the issue.

ME:          I'll drill into processing. The sub-heads I'd examine are rent, electricity, machinery,
             labour and wages, technology, capacity utilisation, packaging, and defects. Is there
             one of these that's the problem?

INTERVIEWER: Yes — capacity utilisation.

ME:          Capacity utilisation is actual output divided by potential output. Are we currently
             at 100% of capacity?

INTERVIEWER: Yes, 100%.

ME:          Then the problem isn't under-utilisation — it's that our *potential* capacity is too
             low. How many plants and machines do we have?

INTERVIEWER: Three plants, each with 30,000-bottle capacity, all at 100%. Advanced machines
             cost 1.5× but produce twice as much.

ME:          So we're capped at a low ceiling because of older machines. My recommendations:
             short-term, source similar higher-capacity machines from other vendors/countries;
             medium-term, lobby the government to relax import restrictions; long-term, design
             and develop our own technology or license it and build in India.

INTERVIEWER: That's great. We can close the case here.
```

## 6. Beer Manufacturer — the book's model solution (Page 35)
- **Recommendations:** a combination of short-term and long-term.
  - Short-term: look for similar vendors in other countries (even paying a bit more is fine, since scaling production covers it).
  - Medium-term: lobby the government to relax import restrictions.
  - Long-term: design/develop own technology, or acquire IP/licences and build in India.
- **Key observations:** establish the major cost buckets quickly; break each cost component into multiple levers; when analysing a cost bucket, look at components in decreasing order of magnitude.

# PART C — THE INTRODUCTORY CASE: "CAMPUS CANTEEN" (Pages 30-32, Easy)
The book uses this simple case to show that **the same problem can be solved two different ways** — proof that there's no single "right" answer.

> "A small canteen in the IIMA campus has witnessed a dip in its revenues. Help them recognise the root cause and give recommendations to grow their revenues."

**Approach 1 (Revenue breakdown):**
- Revenue = # of orders × Average Order Value (AOV).
- Orders are down; AOV is up; customers unchanged → focus on orders.
- Orders = demand side (need, awareness, accessibility, affordability, acceptability) OR supply side (procurement, preparation, distribution).
- Demand factors all look fine (even improving — they started delivery).
- Supply side: the store started delivering food; one of the 3 night-shift employees walks to dorms to deliver → less staff to prepare food → longer wait times → fewer dine-in orders.
- **Recommendations:** add equipment to process orders simultaneously; hire one more night-shift employee; increase the delivery fee to compensate for lost dine-in revenue.

**Approach 2 (Customer journey):**
- Break the customer's journey into before / during / after the visit.
- Complaints are rising *during* the visit → break into product factors and service factors.
- Product unchanged → service issue → wait time is the problem → same root cause.

**Key lesson:** Approach 2 pinpoints the issue more precisely; Approach 1 gets there faster. **Both are correct.** This is the mindset you need — pick a structure and drive it to a conclusion.

# PART D — MARKET ENTRY CASES
*(Book Section B — 11 cases, e.g. Production House, Online Dating App, EV Manufacturer, Fintech in UAE)*

## 1. The concept in very simple language
**What is a market entry case?** The client is thinking about entering a *new market* — a new country, a new product category, a new customer group — and you must decide whether they *should* enter and, if so, *how*.

**The Market Entry Framework (book Page 16):**
```
Is it worthwhile entering?   (economic + strategic attractiveness)
   └─ Customer: segments, needs, size & growth, target group
   └─ Company: product mix, resources, key assets, value chain feasibility
   └─ Competition: # competitors, market share, barriers to entry/exit, gap in expectations
If yes, HOW to enter?
   └─ Greenfield (build from scratch) / Acquisition (buy a competitor) /
      Joint Venture (partner) / Outsourcing
```

**Key terms:**
- **Market Size:** total possible value/sales in the market (e.g. `Revenue = # customers × price`).
- **Market Share:** the % of that market you can capture.
- **Greenfield:** building a new business from scratch — expensive, slower, but full control.
- **Acquisition:** buying an existing player — faster, gets you customers/tech instantly.
- **Joint Venture (JV):** partnering with a local/other firm to share resources and risk.

**Why we use it:** It forces you to answer the *two* central questions the book highlights — *"Is it worthwhile entering the market (economically and strategically) and if yes, what would be the best way to enter?"*

**When to use it in an interview:** Any case with words like "should we enter", "launch in a new country", "should we build or buy", "new product launch".

## 2. HOW TO APPROACH IT IN AN INTERVIEW
1. **Clarify the objective first** — *always* ask why they want to enter and what the goal is (profit? market share? defence?).
   - *"What is the client's objective in entering this market — is it to maximise long-term profit, gain market share, or something strategic?"*
2. **Understand the company:** what it does, history, why this market/product.
3. **Structure the three lenses:** market attractiveness (size/growth/competition), financial/economic viability (can we make a profit at achievable share), operational feasibility (can we actually set up?).
   - *"I'd like to approach this in three parts: first, assess the market attractiveness; second, test the economic viability; and third, check operational feasibility. Does that sound fair?"*
4. **Analyse:** size the market (population → segmentation → affordability → adoption), assess competition, then decide on the *mode of entry*.
5. **Conclude:** a clear go/no-go plus the recommended entry method.

## 3. What the interviewer is testing & common mistakes
- **Testing:** your ability to combine market sizing, competitor analysis, and strategic judgement.
- **Mistake 1:** jumping to "yes/no" without sizing the market. Always quantify.
- **Mistake 2:** forgetting the *mode of entry* decision — a partner may be needed.
- **Mistake 3:** assuming facts (population, prices) — the book says: *"Assume numbers that are easy for calculation and confirm them with the interviewer."*
## 4. A real case from the book — "Production House" (Page 86, Easy)
**The interviewer presents:**
> "Your client is a large production house based out of Andhra Pradesh that produces the best Tollywood movies. They are looking to create an OTT platform where they can host their movies. The client has consulted you to advise them whether they should do so or not."

**Step-by-step thinking:**

| Step | What you do | What you're thinking |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| 1 | Confirm the objective: *"What is the client's objective in creating this OTT platform?"* | Framing matters. If the goal were just "stop piracy," the answer might be different than "grow revenue." |
| 2 | Clarify scope: platform for only their movies or all Telugu content? Budget-friendly or premium? | These answers change both market size and pricing logic. |
| 3 | **Structure:** market attractiveness → financial viability → operational viability. | This mirrors the book's market entry framework: *should we* (worth it?) then *how* (feasible?). |
| 4 | **Size the market:** Telugu-speaking population ≈ 15 crore → 3 crore households (5/household). Split by income: low 40%, medium 40%, high 20% → affordability: 20% / 50% / 100% → 2.2 crore affordable households. | Top-down demand approach: population → households → income/filters. |
| 5 | Apply awareness (80%) and adoption (40%) filters → 0.7 crore expected subscribers. | "Awareness depends on marketing; adoption depends on competition." |
| 6 | **Price it:** competitor + value based. Netflix ≈ ₹499, Hotstar ≈ ₹150 (cap). Budget positioning → ₹50/month. | Price must be consistent with the client's budget-friendly objective. |
| 7 | **Revenue:** 0.7 crore × ₹50 × 12 = ₹420 crore/year. | Always convert to annual revenue — credibility check. |
| 8 | **Mode of entry:** greenfield vs acquire vs outsource. Recommend **acquisition** (no IT capabilities, control matters). | Weighs cost, speed, control. |

## 5. The case as an actual interview conversation (from the book, lightly adapted)
```
INTERVIEWER: The client is a Tollywood production house considering creating its own OTT
             platform. Advise whether they should.

ME:          To confirm — the client produces Tollywood movies and wants to decide whether to
             build their own OTT platform. May I ask a few clarifying questions first?

INTERVIEWER: Go ahead.

ME:          What is the objective behind the platform — and is it meant only for their own
             movies, or wider content?

INTERVIEWER: They want to generate more revenue — most of their movies are pirated and earn
             nothing after theatre release. They are open to hosting other production houses'
             content too, aimed at the Telugu-speaking audience, and they want it budget-friendly.

ME:          Understood. I'd like to structure my approach in three parts: market attractiveness,
             economic feasibility, and operational feasibility. Does that sound reasonable?

INTERVIEWER: Yes. Let's start with the market size.

ME:          The Telugu-speaking population in AP and Telangana is about 15 crore. At 5 people
             per household, that's 3 crore households — one subscription per family. If I split
             households by income — 40% low, 40% medium, 20% high — and assume affordability
             of 20%, 50%, and 100% respectively, that gives roughly 2.2 crore affordable
             households. Are these splits reasonable?

INTERVIEWER: Yes. Go ahead.

ME:          Next, awareness and adoption. As a large production house, awareness could be ~80%;
             given strong competition from Aha, Hotstar, Netflix, Zee5, adoption might be ~40%.
             That gives 0.7 crore expected subscribers.

INTERVIEWER: Good. How would you price it?

ME:          I'd use competitor-based and value-based pricing. Netflix is ~₹499/month, Hotstar
             ~₹150. Since the client wants budget-friendly access for a wide audience, ₹50/month
             seems fair. That gives 0.7 crore × ₹50 = ₹35 crore/month ≈ ₹420 crore/year —
             attractive. So I'd say yes, enter — and for the mode, since the client lacks IT
             capabilities, I'd recommend acquiring an existing platform rather than building or
             outsourcing. That gets speed and control.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you. We can close the case here.
```

# PART E — PRICING CASES
*(Book Section C — 7 cases, e.g. Helicopter Service, Superspeed Travel, 5G Plan, Drug Pricing)*

## 1. The concept in very simple language
**What is a pricing case?** The client needs you to decide *how much to charge* for a product or service (often a brand-new one with no existing price).

**The Pricing Framework (book Page 18) — the three main methods:**
1. **Cost-Based Pricing:** price = all your costs + a profit margin. Good as a *minimum floor* (never price below cost). Uses fixed costs, variable costs, and break-even analysis.
2. **Competitor-Based Pricing:** price = anchored to what competitors/alternatives charge. Good when similar products exist (reference price).
3. **Value-Based Pricing:** price = based on how much value the customer gets — their willingness to pay. Best for new/differentiated products.

**Key terms:**
- **Break-Even Point:** the point where revenue = cost (no profit, no loss). *"How much must I sell to stop losing money?"*
- **Willingness to Pay (WTP):** the max a customer is willing to pay for the benefit they get.
- **Fixed Costs:** don't change with output (rent, salaries, airport charges).
- **Variable Costs:** change with output (fuel, per-trip costs).
- **Proxies:** when you don't have a true value, you *approximate* it using a similar thing. (E.g., to judge the value of time saved, compare to a cab ride.)

**Why we use it:** Different objectives need different methods. The book (Page 18): *"Depending on objectives of profitability or market share or breaking even, recommend an appropriate approach."*

**When to use it in an interview:** Any case asking "how should we price this?" or "is this viable?"

## 2. HOW TO APPROACH IT IN AN INTERVIEW
1. **Clarify the pricing objective:** maximise profit? gain market share? break even? premium or budget positioning?
2. **Understand product + nature:** is it a new invention, similar to an existing product, or a modification? (Your method flow depends on this.)
3. **Lay out all three methods** and say which you'll use and why.
   - *"There are three ways to price this — cost-based, competitor-based, and value-based. I'd like to estimate the cost floor first, then the value-based ceiling, and see if there's a viable price in between."*
4. **Do the math:** 
   - Cost-based → compute fixed + variable costs → break-even price.
   - Value-based → find what the benefit is worth to customers (use proxies).
5. **Conclude:** recommend a price + whether the business is viable.

## 3. What the interviewer is testing & common mistakes
- **Testing:** whether you know *which* method fits, and whether you tie price to value and costs — not just a guess.
- **Mistake:** blindly pricing low without checking costs. **Always compute the cost floor** — if the value you can charge is below your cost, the business isn't viable (as in the Helicopter case below).
- **Brownie points:** the book highlights *creative proxies* for willingness to pay.
## 4. A real case from the book — "Helicopter Service" (Page 110, Moderate)
**The interviewer presents:**
> "Your client is a Heli air service provider. How would you price this service? Is it a viable business?"

**Worked cost calculation — explained step-by-step:**

First, gather the fixed monthly costs:
- Helicopter rent: **₹16 Lakh/month**
- Airport charges: **₹2 Lakh/month**
- Employee salaries: **₹4 Lakh/month**
- Other overheads: **₹1 Lakh/month**
- **Total fixed cost = 16 + 2 + 4 + 1 = ₹23 Lakh/month**

Now the variable cost (fuel):
- Helicopter uses **60 litres/hour**, fuel at **₹75/litre** → ₹4,500/hour.
- A trip needs to go to both ends of the city; one full trip (there + back) takes 1 hour of flying. Because we alternate between two ports, each round trip actually takes **2.5 hours** (1 hour flying + break for pilot/helicopter) before the next trip.
- So we can do **4 round trips per day** (10 hours usable / 2.5 hours per trip = 4), i.e. 8 hours of actual flying per day.
- Variable cost per day = 8 hours × ₹4,500 = **₹36,000/day**.
- Operating **25 days/month** → variable cost = 25 × 36,000 = **₹9 Lakh/month**.

Total monthly cost = Fixed ₹23 Lakh + Variable ₹9 Lakh = **₹32 Lakh/month**.
- 4 trips/day × 25 days = **100 trips/month**.
- **Break-even price per trip = ₹32 Lakh / 100 trips = ₹32,000 per trip.**

That's the cost floor. Now test value:
- A cab from the airport to the city takes ~2 hours and costs ~₹800.
- The helicopter takes 1 hour (saves ~1 hour). For a customer to pay ~₹32,000, their time would need to be worth ~₹31,000+/hour — unrealistic.
- Even a 60-minute ride valued against premium services is ~₹8,000–13,000, still far below the ₹32,000 cost.

**Conclusion:** The cost floor (₹32,000/trip) is far above what customers would pay for the value/time saved. **The business is NOT viable.**

## 5. The case as an actual interview conversation (condensed from the book)
```
INTERVIEWER: A Heli air service client wants you to price the service. Is it viable?

ME:          I'd like to start by understanding the service and its costs. What is the operating
             model?

INTERVIEWER: One helicopter in Delhi, airport to two city ports, no existing competitor.

ME:          Let me set out the costing. What are the fixed costs?

INTERVIEWER: Rent ₹16L/month, airport ₹2L, salaries ₹4L, overheads ₹1L.

ME:          That's ₹23 lakh fixed per month. Variable costs — what's fuel consumption?

INTERVIEWER: 60 litres/hour at ₹75/litre.

ME:          A full round trip takes 2.5 hours (flying plus breaks), so 4 trips per day = 8 hours
             flying = 8 × 60 × 75 = ₹36,000/day in fuel. Over 25 operating days that's ₹9 lakh
             per month. Total cost = 32 lakh/month. With 100 trips, break-even is ₹32,000 per trip.

INTERVIEWER: How does that compare to the value?

ME:          A cab costs ~₹800 and takes 2 hours; the helicopter saves ~1 hour. To justify
             ₹32,000, the customer's hour would have to be worth well over ₹30,000 — that's
             only true for people who could own a helicopter themselves. So value is far below
             cost. My recommendation: at this scale, the service is not viable —
             the price needed to break even is too high for the value delivered.
```

# PART F — GROWTH STRATEGY CASES
*(Book Section D — 11 cases, e.g. Apparel Manufacturer, Elevator, E-commerce, Fertilizer)*

## 1. The concept in very simple language
**What is a growth case?** The client wants to grow — usually by a specific % year-on-year — and you must figure out *where the growth will come from* and *how to achieve it*.

**The Growth Framework (book Page 19) — think of it as a "growth tree":**
```
Growth can come from:
├── ORGANIC (internal growth)
│   ├── Revenue per user: sell more per customer (upsell, bundling, price, loyalty)
│   │   └── Revenue = # users × # transactions/user × revenue/transaction
│   └── # of users: more customers
│       ├── in existing markets (cross-sell, better marketing, new channels)
│       └── in new markets (new segments, new geographies)
└── INORGANIC (external growth)
    ├── Acquisitions (buy another company)
    └── Joint Ventures (partner)
```

**Key terms:**
- **Organic growth:** growing through your own effort (sell more, new products, new markets).
- **Inorganic growth:** growing by buying or partnering (M&A, JV).
- **Ansoff Matrix (book Page 25):** a simple 2×2 to think about growth options:
  - Existing product + existing market = **Market Penetration** (least risky)
  - Existing product + new market = **Market Development**
  - New product + existing market = **Product Development**
  - New product + new market = **Diversification** (riskiest)

**Why we use it:** The book (Page 19) says a growth scenario usually has a specific target (e.g. 20% YoY). You must *align on the target, validate it, identify the pillars that can deliver it, and recommend how to leverage them.*

## 2. HOW TO APPROACH IT IN AN INTERVIEW
1. **Clarify the goal:** *"What's the growth target — revenue, users, or both — and over what period?"*
2. **Break growth down:** *"I'd like to decompose this into Revenue = # users × # transactions/user × revenue per transaction, and then look at which is most realistic to move."*
3. **Brainstorm pillars:** for each lever, explore channels, marketing, new segments, new products, inorganic options.
4. **Recommend 3 priority initiatives** with reasoning.

## 3. What the interviewer is testing
- Whether you can **structure** growth into concrete levers rather than saying "grow more."
- Creativity in *new channels/segments* earns brownie points (book, Page 19).

# PART F2 — GUESSTIMATES
*(Book Section G — 22 guesstimates)*

## 1. The concept in very simple language
**What is a guesstimate?** *"A combination of guess and estimate"* (book Page 241). You estimate something you don't know (market size, ad seconds, bricks in a campus) using **structure + reasonable assumptions + back-of-the-envelope math**. It's never about the exact answer — it's about the *approach*.

**Two approaches (book Page 241):**
- **Top-Down:** start big (e.g. India's 140 crore population) and apply filters (rural/urban, gender, age, income) to drill down to your answer.
- **Bottom-Up:** start small (one representative unit, e.g. one shop's sales) and extrapolate up.

**Common splits/filters (book):** Rural-Urban split, Gender split, Age split, Income split.

## 2. HOW TO APPROACH IT IN AN INTERVIEW
1. **Clarify:** *"Are we looking at India or a specific city? A day, a month, or a year?"*
2. **Pick an approach** and state it: *"I'll take a top-down approach — starting from population and applying filters."*
3. **Build a formula** out loud.
4. **State your assumptions** and confirm with the interviewer — *always* confirm made-up numbers.
5. **Do the math cleanly** on the sheet.
6. **Sanity-check** the final number at the end.

## 3. A worked book example — "Gold Flake Cigarettes in Mumbai" (Page 242)
**Question:** *Estimate the annual demand for Gold Flake cigarettes in Mumbai.*

| Step | Calculation | Logic |
|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Start: Mumbai population = **20 million** | Top-down starting point. |
| 2 | Of smoking age: 75% → **15 million** | Adults vs children split. |
| 3 | Smokers: 50% → **7.5 million** | Assumption, stated out loud. |
| 4 | Consumption: 1 pack/day × 365 days × 20 cigs/pack | Smoking rate assumption. |
| 5 | Total cigarette market = 7.5M × 365 × 20 = **54,750 million cigs/year** | Compute total demand. |
| 6 | Gold Flake share ≈ 20% (like Delhi/Bangalore) → **10,950 million cigs/year** | Apply brand share to get the answer. |

Every number above was an assumption the candidate *stated and confirmed* — that's what matters, not whether 50% or 60% is "right".

## 4. Interviewer's expectations for guesstimates
- **What looks strong:** clear structure, formula first, reasonable assumptions confirmed out loud, clean arithmetic, sanity check.
- **What looks weak:** staring silently, random numbers, forgetting to state assumptions, no formula.

# PART G — YOUR "THINKING TEMPLATE" FOR ANY UNSEEN CASE
This is the mental process to apply to a *brand-new* case you've never seen. Memorise the *logic*, not the answer.

## The 6-step mental loop (adapt to ANY case)
```
1. "WHAT'S THE EXACT QUESTION?"   → Confirm client, objective, timeframe. ASK before assuming.
2. "WHICH FRAMEWORK FITS?"        → Profitability / Market entry / Pricing / Growth / Guesstimate.
                                   Pick the lens just to START. Don't force it — adapt to the problem.
3. "HOW DO I SPLIT IT?"           → Build a tree (e.g. Revenue = Price × Qty; Growth = users ×
                                   transactions × revenue). State the formula OUT LOUD.
4. "WHAT DO I KNOW vs NEED?"      → List facts you have. For what you need, ASK the interviewer
                                   or make a reasonable assumption and CONFIRM it.
5. "WHAT'S THE NUMBER / INSIGHT?" → Do the math cleanly. For every data point ask:
                                   "how did this look earlier for us?" and "how does it look for competitors?"
6. "SO WHAT?" (Conclude)          → Give a clear recommendation + short and long-term actions.
                                   Sanity-check: does the number make sense?
```

## Power phrases to have ready
- *"Before I dive in, I'd like to confirm a few things..."*
- *"Let me structure this as..."*
- *"I'll take a top-down / bottom-up approach, starting from..."*
- *"I'm going to make an assumption here that X, and confirm it with you..."*
- *"How did this look for us earlier, and for our competitors?"*
- *"To conclude, I recommend X in the short term and Y in the long term."*

## The divider question that earns points (book Page 11)
Ask *one* of these early — they instantly show strategic thinking:
- **Profitability:** *"Is this a problem specific to the client, or is it an industry-wide trend?"*
- **Market entry:** *"What is the client's objective for entering?"*
- **Growth:** *"Is growth expected organically or does the client consider acquisitions?"*
- **Pricing:** *"What's the pricing objective — profit, market share, or break-even?"*

## Final checklist before you recommend
- [ ] Did I confirm the problem statement? (Don't solve the wrong problem.)
- [ ] Did I use a clear structure, not random ideas?
- [ ] Did I state and confirm my assumptions for any number?
- [ ] Did I analyse costs AND benefits / revenue AND costs?
- [ ] Is my recommendation concrete, with short-term and long-term actions?
- [ ] Did I sanity-check my final number?

## ONE-OVERVIEW REFERENCE — The book's frameworks at a glance
| Case type | Primary framework | First thing to clarify |
|-----------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Profitability | Profit = Revenue − Cost; costs along Value Chain | Cost or revenue? Client vs industry? |
| Market entry | Customer / Company / Competition + mode of entry | Objective for entering |
| New product intro | Financing + Value chain + Profit/Break-even | Objective for launching |
| Pricing | Cost / Competitor / Value based | Pricing objective |
| Growth | Revenue = users × transactions × revenue; Ansoff matrix | Growth target & timeline |
| M&A / Due diligence | Financial + Non-financial benefits vs costs; market/competition/business/customer | Acquirer's objective & profile |
| Guesstimate | Top-down / bottom-up with filters | Geography + time period |

**Remember the book's core advice (Page 29):** there is no single right answer — the interviewer wants to see *how you think*. Be structured, be confident, communicate constantly, and you will nail any case. Good luck!
